Q1. Why can Treponema pallidum grow only in microaerophilic or anaerobic conditions and in cell culture?
Answer: It lacks a free radical scavenger system (catalase/superoxide dismutase), cannot perform the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and depends directly on host cell substrates.
Explanation: Treponema pallidum has a highly reduced genome. Because it lacks protective enzymes against toxic reactive oxygen species and is metabolically defective in terminal oxidative phosphorylation and the Krebs cycle, it relies strictly on host cell metabolites provided under low-oxygen conditions.
Q2. What laboratory methods are used for the identification of Chlamydia trachomatis?
Answer: Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT), cell culture isolation (using McCoy, HeLa 229, HL, or Hep-2 cells), and serological antibody testing.
Explanation: NAAT is currently the gold standard due to its superior sensitivity and specificity. Tissue cell culture (e.g., cycloheximide-treated McCoy cells) detects characteristic glycogen-containing intracytoplasmic elementary bodies. Serology can help diagnose invasive systemic syndromes like Lymphogranuloma Venereum (LGV).
Q3. Why can Bacillus anthracis be recognized under microscopic examination?
Answer: Due to its characteristic long chains of large, square-ended Gram-positive bacilli resembling "bamboo canes" or "boxcars".
Explanation: B. anthracis forms prominent chains of large, non-motile rods with flat ends that give a classic segmented bamboo-stalk appearance on Gram stain and blood smears.
Q4. How can Leptospira be detected in clinical specimens?
Answer: Dark-field microscopy and Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR).
Explanation: Because Leptospira spirochetes are too slender (0.1 µm wide) to be resolved by standard bright-field microscopy, dark-field illumination is required to observe their rapid spinning motility with hooked ends. PCR provides early diagnostic confirmation during the initial leptospiremic phase.
Q5. Describe the standard therapeutic regimen for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
Answer: Initial intensive phase with Isoniazid (INH), Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, and Ethambutol for 2 months, followed by a continuation phase of INH and Rifampin for 4–6 months.
Explanation: This multi-drug regimen (RIPE) is mandatory to prevent the emergence of drug-resistant mutants and to eradicate slow-growing intracellular mycobacteria across cavitary, granulomatous, and caseous lesions.
Q6. What virulence factors of Streptococcus pneumoniae mediate tissue destruction?
Answer: Pneumococcal teichoic acid, peptidoglycan fragments, and pneumolysin phosphorylcholine.
Explanation: Teichoic acid and peptidoglycan fragments induce intense local complement activation and leukocyte infiltration, while pneumolysin creates transmembrane pores in epithelial and endothelial host cells, causing direct cytotoxicity.
Q7. Why can Streptococcus pyogenes avoid host opsonization and phagocytosis?
Answer: Through its hyaluronic acid capsule, M proteins, M-like proteins, and C5a peptidase.
Explanation: The hyaluronic acid capsule mimics human ground substance (non-immunogenic), M protein degrades complement factor C3b deposition on the bacterial wall, and C5a peptidase cleaves the chemotactic signal C5a, preventing neutrophil recruitment.
Q8. Give at least four characteristics that differentiate Enterobacteriaceae from Pseudomonadaceae.
Answer: Glucose fermentation (+ vs -), Oxidase reaction (- vs +), Flagellar arrangement/ciliature (peritrichous vs monotrichous/polar), and optimum growth temperature/metabolism (facultative anaerobes vs strict aerobes).
Explanation: Enterobacteriaceae are oxidase-negative, ferment glucose, and typically have peritrichous flagella. In contrast, Pseudomonas is oxidase-positive, utilizes glucose oxidatively (non-fermenter), and possesses a single polar flagellum (monotrichous).
Q9. Describe the morphology and name the two important human pathogens of Enterococcus.
Answer: Gram-positive cocci arranged in pairs or short chains, facultatively anaerobic, non-motile, and catalase-negative. The two major species are Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium.
Explanation: E. faecalis accounts for the majority of human clinical isolates, whereas E. faecium is frequently associated with high rates of multi-drug resistance, particularly Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE).
Q10. What are the main clinical features of primary syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum?
Answer: A painless, indurated ulcer (chancre) at the site of inoculation accompanied by painless, firm regional lymphadenopathy.
Explanation: Arising approximately 3 weeks post-exposure, the chancre begins as an erythematous papule that quickly erodes into an ulcer with clean bases and cartilaginous borders. It typically resolves spontaneously within 4 to 6 weeks as spirochetes disseminate systemically.
